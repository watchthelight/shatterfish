# Red team: GSPRT on paired-seed depth differences as the gating test

Conclusion under attack: use Fishtest-style GSPRT (Van den Bergh LLR, regularization, overshoot clamp) on per-seed depth differences, hypotheses in standardized units, pre-registered bounds; report win rate with a CI only; expect a few hundred seed pairs instead of thousands of runs.

All sources below were retrieved on 2026-09-03 via WebSearch/WebFetch in this run. Nothing is asserted from memory.

## Counter-evidence

- FINDING: The chess-pairing precedent the conclusion leans on delivers far less variance reduction than "few hundred vs thousands" implies. Fishtest's own analysis of ~150 LTC tests found the within-pair correlation is only about -0.15, making the correct (pentanomial) variance "about 15% smaller than the naive trinomial variance" and allowing "15% fewer games". That is a 1.18x saving, not a 10x saving. The same issue also states that running SPRT with the wrong (independence) variance model makes the test invalid, i.e. the pairing structure must be modelled correctly or the error guarantees are void. | SOURCE: https://github.com/official-stockfish/fishtest/issues/348 | PUBLISHER: official-stockfish/fishtest (GitHub) | PUB_DATE: 2019-01-08 | STRENGTH: strong

- FINDING: Paired-seed (common random numbers) designs only reduce variance while the two treatment trajectories stay correlated; a 2025 multi-agent simulation study shows that when treatments cause trajectories to diverge (chaotic dynamics, sensitive dependence on initial conditions, larger treatment effects) "correlation collapses and variance reduction vanishes", and it explicitly warns practitioners "cannot assume pairing reduces effective sample size ... without verifying trajectory correlation remains sufficiently high". A roguelike run where two bots make different choices from turn one is exactly this regime: the seed fixes the dungeon layout but not the monster RNG stream after the first divergent action. | SOURCE: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.24145 | PUBLISHER: arXiv (Udit Sharma, "When Does Pairing Seeds Reduce Variance? Evidence from a Multi-Agent Economic Simulation") | PUB_DATE: 2025 (v3) | STRENGTH: strong

- FINDING: Common random numbers in agent-based models can fail outright: CRN "does not always work and can backfire if the simulation creates a negative, rather than positive, correlation", and starting conditions plus seed "affect the type of pseudo-stable behavior over long but finite time horizons", so identically seeded runs still diverge chaotically. The paired-difference estimator's variance can therefore exceed the unpaired one. | SOURCE: https://arxiv.org/html/2409.02086v1 | PUBLISHER: arXiv ("Taming Randomness in Agent-Based Models using Common Random Numbers") | PUB_DATE: 2024-09 | STRENGTH: moderate

- FINDING: Wald's SPRT for normal data requires the variance to be known or fixed in the hypothesis; the sequential t-test variants (Rushton 1950/1952, Hajnal 1961) exist precisely because plugging a running sample variance into the LLR is not the SPRT. Separately, the group-sequential t-test literature reports that the critical values in use "are obtained from normal approximations of t-statistics" and that "normal approximation is not accurate when some sample sizes ... are small", requiring Monte Carlo critical values to hold the nominal type I error. A GSPRT that estimates the variance of depth differences from the first few dozen pairs (many of which will be exactly 0, giving a tiny or degenerate variance estimate) is in the small-sample regime where these approximations are documented to fail. | SOURCE: https://ftp.sun.ac.za/ftp/CRAN/web/packages/sprtt/vignettes/t_test.html and https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17434814/ | PUBLISHER: CRAN sprtt package vignette; Statistics in Medicine via PubMed | PUB_DATE: vignette undated (package snapshot 2022); PubMed article 2007 | STRENGTH: moderate

- FINDING: Fishtest's own documentation only claims that "GSPRT asymptotically behaves like SPRT when the error probabilities approach zero" and that Elo estimation "treats the GSPRT like an SPRT for the drift of a Brownian motion". The guarantees are asymptotic and the Brownian approximation error is not quantified. Fishtest gets away with this because it runs on the order of 10^5 games per test; a "few hundred pairs" test is nowhere near the asymptotic regime the documentation relies on. | SOURCE: https://official-stockfish.github.io/docs/fishtest-wiki/Fishtest-Mathematics.html | PUBLISHER: Stockfish docs (Fishtest wiki) | PUB_DATE: undated, current as of 2026-09-03 | STRENGTH: moderate

- FINDING: Fishtest's bounds have been re-litigated because of drift in the underlying outcome distribution: in the SPRT bounds V2 discussion, the share of decisive games had fallen ~30%, invalidating the calibration of the existing bounds; the STC bounds in force at the time had a 12.3% regression (false-pass) rate and a 50% pass point at only 0.5 Elo, and the fix was to retune the bounds at ~5% extra LTC cost. Translating to a roguelike rig: "pre-registered bounds in standardized units" are calibrated to today's depth distribution; every change to the bot or game version (more early deaths, fewer ties at depth 1-3) shifts the effective operating characteristics and silently changes the false-pass rate. | SOURCE: https://github.com/glinscott/fishtest/issues/865 | PUBLISHER: glinscott/fishtest (GitHub) | PUB_DATE: 2020-11-12 | STRENGTH: moderate

- FINDING: An independent simulation comparison of sequential tests found SPRT reached only ~73% power versus 83% for a fixed-sample test and 80% for a group-sequential (AGILE) design under identical conditions, i.e. "roughly 61% higher type II error", and needed 22% larger samples than AGILE to hit 80% power; its 37% average-sample-size saving came "at a highly disproportionate cost in terms of statistical power". SPRT's performance depends on the true effect matching the hypothesized alternative, which for an untested new bot metric is unknown. | SOURCE: https://blog.analytics-toolkit.com/2022/comparison-of-the-statistical-power-of-sequential-tests/ | PUBLISHER: Analytics-Toolkit.com (Georgi Georgiev) | PUB_DATE: 2022, updated 2023-03-31 | STRENGTH: moderate

- FINDING: Anytime-valid alternatives exist that do not require a pre-specified alternative effect size or a fixed stopping rule: mixture SPRT (mSPRT, adopted by Uber and Netflix per the retrieved sources) and e-value based tests control type I error "at every observation" and permit data-driven stopping without pre-registering bounds; an e-value sequential test reduces to SPRT only when no acceptance bound is set. Choosing Wald/GSPRT over these forgoes the ability to look, continue, and revise the alternative hypothesis without invalidating the test. | SOURCE: https://arxiv.org/html/2606.00878 and https://arxiv.org/pdf/1512.04922 | PUBLISHER: arXiv ("Anytime-valid testing with e-values and confirmatory adaptive designs"; Johari et al. "Always Valid Inference") | PUB_DATE: 2026-06; 2015-12 | STRENGTH: moderate

- FINDING: Heavily tied ordinal differences are a documented problem for paired rank/mean tests: with many ties the Wilcoxon signed-rank test "cannot compute the exact p-value and falls back to a normal approximation", and ties above ~15-20% of observations reduce power and "may indicate inappropriate use of the test"; a separate methods paper argues tied observations must be handled explicitly (omitted) for the two-sample t and WMW tests to behave. Depth differences between two bots that both mostly die on floors 1-5 will be tied (exactly zero) far more than 20% of the time. | SOURCE: https://mcpanalytics.ai/articles/wilcoxon-signed-rank-test-practical-guide-for-data-driven-decisions and https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6057651/ | PUBLISHER: MCP Analytics; PMC (NIH) | PUB_DATE: undated (2024-25); 2018 | STRENGTH: weak

- FINDING: The NetHack Challenge organisers report that a single scalar progress proxy (in-game score) was gamed: "many entrants elected to camp in the early stages of the dungeon, grinding out a high score ... instead of progressing", and they conclude that "future challenges should focus on refining evaluation metrics", possibly via "checkpointing of achievements", rather than one number. This partly supports depth over score, but it is direct evidence that whatever scalar is gated on becomes the optimisation target; the paper does not validate depth as a win-rate proxy, and a depth-gated bot has the mirror incentive (dive, skip preparation, die deeper). | SOURCE: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.11889 | PUBLISHER: arXiv ("Insights From the NeurIPS 2021 NetHack Challenge") | PUB_DATE: 2022-03 | STRENGTH: moderate

## Strongest argument the conclusion is wrong

The conclusion imports Fishtest's machinery without Fishtest's operating conditions. Fishtest's GSPRT guarantees are explicitly asymptotic (error probabilities to zero, Brownian-drift approximation) and are exercised at roughly 10^5 paired games per test, with a pairing structure (same opening, colours reversed) that yields only a -0.15 within-pair correlation and a 15% variance saving (issue #348). The proposed rig expects the same test to be valid at a few hundred pairs on a bounded, integer, zero-inflated metric whose variance must be estimated from the same early data used to stop; the sequential-t literature says normal/asymptotic approximations are inaccurate exactly there (PubMed 17434814; sprtt vignette). The "few hundred pairs" claim also assumes pairing buys a large variance reduction, but the CRN literature (arXiv 2512.24145, 2409.02086) shows that benefit collapses when trajectories diverge, and in a roguelike two different policies diverge from the first decision, so the seed fixes little beyond the initial layout; the honest prior is closer to chess's 15% than to a 10x saving, and it must be measured, not assumed. Meanwhile Fishtest's own history shows pre-registered bounds drift with the outcome distribution (issue #865, 12.3% regression rate at STC before retuning), and independent simulations show SPRT's sample savings come with materially lower power than fixed or group-sequential designs when the true effect is not the hypothesised one. Finally, gating on depth converts it into the optimisation target; the NetHack Challenge report is a documented case of a scalar proxy steering entrants away from the real objective. A team that adopts this design is likely, in six months, to be (a) debugging false passes on regressions that die deeper, (b) arguing about re-tuning bounds after every engine or game-version change, and (c) discovering that their "few hundred pairs" estimate was off by an order of magnitude once trajectory correlation is measured. A fixed-sample or group-sequential paired test with a permutation/bootstrap reference distribution (or an e-value/mSPRT design that does not need a pre-registered alternative) on a composite outcome (win, then depth, then turns survived) would be the more defensible default.

## Searched and not found

- A simulation study specifically evaluating GSPRT / Fishtest LLR on bounded, discrete, zero-inflated ordinal differences and reporting realised type I error versus nominal. Searched "GSPRT generalized sequential probability ratio test non-normal discrete data simulation type I error exceeds nominal"; results were theoretical GSPRT papers (Kiefer-Weiss 1957, Lai) and non-parametric confidence-sequence work, none with the requested empirical failure numbers.
- Direct evidence from roguelike bots (NetHack, DCSS, Pixel Dungeon / Shattered Pixel Dungeon) that a bot optimised for dungeon depth reached ends up with a lower win rate. Searched "NetHack Challenge NLE dungeon depth proxy metric agents dive deeper but win less reward hacking"; the NetHack Challenge report documents score-gaming and recommends depth-like progress metrics, but no source was found that measured a depth-vs-win trade-off.
- A documented case of a Stockfish patch passing SPRT and later being reverted as a regression, with numbers. The bounds V2 issue gives modelled regression rates (12.3% / 1.2%) but no catalogue of realised false passes was retrieved.
- Any source quantifying seed-pair trajectory correlation in a roguelike or grid-world RL environment specifically. The closest retrieved evidence is the multi-agent economic simulation (arXiv 2512.24145) and the agent-based-model CRN paper (arXiv 2409.02086).
