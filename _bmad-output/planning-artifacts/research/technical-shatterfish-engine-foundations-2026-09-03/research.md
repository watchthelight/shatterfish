# Technical research: Shatterfish engine foundations

## Executive summary

**Decision served.** Ground the product brief, the PRD's non-functional requirements, and the architecture spine (E1 harness, E3 rig, E6 search, E5 overlay) before committing the roadmap.

**What the evidence says to do.**

1. **Build E1 as a headless *scene*, not a scene-less boot.** libGDX's headless backend is a sound substrate (real files and preferences, mocked audio and input, no GL, eager natives load), but at the pinned tag every attack, zap, throw, and operate action resolves inside a sprite-animation callback that only a scene update fires, and game logic holds 969 sprite dereferences. The cheapest fair design is a harness-owned scene driven by fast-forwarding `scene.update()` until the actor thread parks (components in Recommendation 1). Parallelism is one process per game; classloader isolation must keep libGDX in a shared parent loader because JNI forbids loading a native library into two loaders. (Sections 1, 7; contrary evidence A.)
2. **Gate the rig on a Fishtest-style Generalized SPRT (GSPRT) over paired-seed differences of a composite per-run outcome, after measuring what pairing buys.** The GSPRT is metric-agnostic and 20 lines to port; win rate near zero is a poor gate. But chess pairing saves only about 15%, the approximations are asymptotic, and whatever is gated becomes the target, so the rig must measure pair correlation, use a burn-in and simulated calibration before stopping, gate on a composite (win, then depth, then turns), and evaluate an e-process alternative in the E3 ADR. (Section 3; contrary evidence B.)
3. **Keep the brain symbolic-first, with the costs priced in.** The 2021 NetHack Challenge and everything since (BALROG 2026: every LLM below 7% NetHack progression) show hand-written, hierarchical, belief-table bots ahead by a wide margin; their arbitration is a priority list of interruptible behaviors with cheap entry predicates, and combat is one-step scoring. The costs are real: symbolic bots plateau, rule stacks go opaque, and Shatterfish's observation-only rule adds a perception layer AutoAscend never built. Mitigate with playbooks as data, a strategy log in the overlay, and an annual review of the learned frontier. (Sections 4, 5; contrary evidence C.)
4. **Search: measure before choosing.** Plain redetermination is the Perfect Information Monte Carlo (PIMC) pattern whose pathologies (strategy fusion, non-locality) are well documented; whether they bite depends on leaf correlation and disambiguation, which nobody has measured for a roguelike. Item identification is the poker-like regime and belongs to belief reasoning; tactics are plausibly the benign regime. E6 starts with one-ply lookahead over Codex tables, then measures Long et al.'s properties and the simulator's turns per second before committing to sampled search. (Section 4.)
5. **Overlay and boundary enforcement are low-risk.** The native UI toolkit has everything a docked panel needs (`Component`, `Chrome.TOAST_TR`, `renderTextBlock`, `ScrollPane`, `SPDAction`), with two small hooks and one hard rule: all panel text is set on the render thread, because `RenderedText.measure()` throws on the actor thread. ArchUnit 1.5.0 is current and maintained; keep it as one of three boundary layers and bump from 1.3.0. (Sections 2, 7.)

**What the numbered items don't cover.** No bot, RL agent, or gym exists for Shattered Pixel Dungeon (SPD), and no agent of any architecture has surpassed AutoAscend on NetHack: Shatterfish enters an empty field with the seedfinder lineage (invisible-window LWJGL3 boots) as its only precedent. (Section 6.)

**Biggest caveat.** Two central numbers are unmeasured and cannot be researched: the variance reduction paired seeds buy in this game, and the disambiguation and leaf correlation of its tactics. Both are E1/E3 measurements with published recipes; the roadmap must not assume either.

**Upstream timing.** The `4.0.0-beta` tag is the v3.3.8 commit; upstream master has been silent since March 2026 while 4.0 is developed privately. Expect a single large drop of content-heavy, not structural, changes; measure the merge with one API call when 4.0 stable lands.

## 1. libGDX headless backend

**Question.** Can Shattered Pixel Dungeon's core be driven on libGDX's headless backend without a scene, what breaks, and how can many games run per machine?

**Verdict (amended after the red-team pass; see Contrary evidence).** Right substrate, wrong boot: at v3.3.8, attack, zap, throw, and operate resolve inside sprite-animation callbacks that only a scene update fires, and game logic holds 969 sprite dereferences [130] [126], so "never construct a scene" is not viable. E1 becomes a **headless scene** (component list in Recommendation 1), with upstream edits limited to the few static helpers that assume a `GameScene`. Parallelism is per process by default; classloader isolation must keep libGDX in a shared parent loader (the JNI one-loader rule) and is an E1 spike whose exit criterion is a measured throughput number.

**What the backend is.** `HeadlessApplication` was contributed as a mocked backend for servers sharing client logic; its `MockGraphics` header says as much [35]. Its constructor loads natives, installs a logger, creates real `HeadlessFiles` and `HeadlessNet` and mock graphics, audio, and input, assigns the process-global `Gdx.app/files/net/audio/graphics/input`, and starts a dedicated main-loop thread; it never assigns `Gdx.gl`, and every GL getter on `MockGraphics` returns null [34] [35]. `HeadlessApplicationConfiguration` has three fields; `updatesPerSecond` of 0 gives a busy loop with no sleep (maximum throughput) and a negative value means `render()` is never called, so a harness would drive the game itself through posted runnables [36]. `exit()` is asynchronous: it posts a runnable that ends the loop on the next iteration [34].

**What breaks.** Anything that constructs a `Texture`, `SpriteBatch`, or `ShaderProgram` dereferences `Gdx.gl` and throws; issue #3383 (2015) records it and is closed without a fix, and master still returns null GL, so this is by design and permanent [42]. Issue #5995 (2020) lists the two community workarounds: Mockito-mocking `Gdx.gl`, or a hidden LWJGL3 window with a real context [43]; a participant on the earlier issue noted that mocking is not enough once code compiles shaders [42]. A 2020 server write-up hit the same NPE from a map loader that auto-created textures and fixed it with a stub image resolver [44]. The community test skeleton boots a `HeadlessApplication` and mocks `GL20` [45]. No published hand-written no-op `GL20` was found in three rounds. Preferences work but write real files under `preferencesDirectory`, so parallel instances sharing one directory would collide [40]; audio calls are safe no-ops [41]; `Timer` is JVM-global and rebinds to whichever `Gdx.files` is current, so it churns and mis-pauses when several applications share a JVM [39].

**Natives.** The constructor calls `HeadlessNativesLoader.load()` first, which delegates to `GdxNativesLoader.load()`; that method has a `disableNativesLoading` flag and otherwise lets any failure propagate [38] [34]. The failure is eager and concrete: the lead reproduced it this run on Windows 11 with libGDX 1.14.0, where constructing a `HeadlessApplication` without `gdx-platform:natives-desktop` on the classpath threw `SharedLibraryLoadRuntimeException: Couldn't load shared library 'gdx64.dll'` with cause "Unable to read file for extraction". Adding the natives as a runtime dependency fixed it [52]. The loader source itself has moved out of the main repository (gdx-jnigen) and was not retrieved, so the exception path is verified empirically, not from source.

**Many games per machine.** Every `HeadlessApplication` overwrites the same `Gdx.*` statics, which libGDX's own javadoc calls "a design faux pas" it accepted deliberately [37]; the wiki says no libGDX class is thread-safe unless marked and that all listener methods run on one thread, and nowhere addresses multiple applications [51]. The LWJGL3 backend's multi-window support does not help: `newWindow` shares one `Gdx.app`, one loop, and the first window's GL context [50]. Consequence: parallel instances mean separate JVM processes, or one classloader per instance so that each loader has its own `Gdx` class; the latter is mechanically feasible for a headless loop (a plain thread with no GL affinity) but has no precedent or maintainer statement and must be spiked. SPD's own statics (`Dungeon`, `Actor`, `Game`) sit on top of libGDX's and impose the same constraint.

**Versions and health.** Releases by GitHub tag date: 1.13.1 (2025-01-08), 1.13.5 (2025-05-13, the Sonatype Central migration), 1.14.0 (2025-10-19), 1.14.1 (2026-05-18), 1.14.2 (2026-06-05) [47]; the libgdx.com posts corroborate the months and the order (1.14.0 posted 2025-10-20; one combined 1.14.1 and 1.14.2 post on 2026-05-28) [46]. libGDX has been built with JDK 17 since 1.13.0 while its published jars target Java 7 (core, headless) and Java 8 (LWJGL3); LWJGL 3.3.3 has been pinned since 1.13.1, with 3.4.x needed only on Java 25+ [48] [49]. gdx-liftoff recommends JDK 21 and targets 1.14.2 [49]. No release note in the trailing year touches the headless backend: it is stable by neglect, which is acceptable for a mock backend whose surface has not changed.

**Confidence.** High on backend behavior (source read on master; the pinned build sets `gdxVersion = '1.14.0'` [133]); high on the natives failure (first-hand reproduction); high on the sprite-coupling finding (verified line by line at v3.3.8); medium on the "no no-op GL exists" absence claim; low on classloader isolation, which is inference without precedent.

## 2. ArchUnit and boundary enforcement

**Question.** Can ArchUnit carry the "brain never depends on game code" invariant for years, and what else should stand beside it?

**Verdict.** ArchUnit 1.5.0 on JUnit 5 is a sound, actively maintained choice with a two-year horizon of predictable releases. It should be one of three layers, not the layer:

1. no declared dependency edge,
2. a resolution-time assertion on the brain's classpaths,
3. the ArchUnit rule against bytecode references.

The project's current pin of 1.3.0 should move to 1.5.0.

**State of the library.** ArchUnit's current release is 1.5.0, published 2026-08-04, preceded by 1.4.2 (2026-04), 1.4.1 (2025-05), 1.4.0 (2025-02); the cadence is roughly two feature releases a year plus backports [1]. Maven Central confirms 1.5.0 as the latest `archunit-junit5` artifact, uploaded the same day [2]. Java 21 class files have been supported throughout the 1.x line; 1.5.0 adds Java 27 class files, an `archunit-junit6` module, sealed-class introspection, and a thread-safe violation store for parallel tests, and its own build moved to Gradle 9 [1]. No Gradle 9 incompatibility was found; ArchUnit is a test-scoped library with no build plugin, so the only Gradle 9 requirement is the explicit `junit-platform-launcher` dependency the project already declares (absence of evidence, medium confidence). The repository shows about 3,800 stars and 154 open issues under an Apache-2.0 license [9].

**The rule shape.** The idiomatic boundary rule is `noClasses().that().resideInAPackage("..brain..").should().dependOnClassesThat().resideInAnyPackage("com.shatteredpixel..", "com.watabou..")`; the allow-list form is `onlyDependOnClassesThat()`. ArchUnit fails by default when a rule selects zero classes, which guards against a silently empty package; `allowEmptyShould(true)` opts out per rule [3]. `FreezingArchRule` records existing violations so only new ones fail, which is for grown codebases and not needed here [3]. Rules keyed on package names stop matching after a rename, so the fail-on-empty default is the companion check [4].

**What it cannot see.** ArchUnit reads bytecode only: reflection, `Class.forName` strings, and resources are invisible to it; classes outside the import scope are resolved from the classpath under `resolveMissingDependenciesFromClassPath` and bounded by `maxIterationsFor*` settings [3]. This is why a bytecode rule alone is not enough for a fairness guarantee: it must sit on a classpath boundary.

**Performance.** `@AnalyzeClasses` caches imports per location across test classes in one JVM [3]. The one open performance complaint, 87 s for a 120-class project, was traced in the issue thread to pulling in classpath classes during dependency resolution rather than to the project's own classes: the reporter's 80 s fell to 1.4 s after a maintainer suggested disabling `resolveMissingDependenciesFromClassPath` [5]; a 2026 production report puts thousands of classes at seconds [6]. For a brain module of a few hundred classes with `DoNotIncludeTests` and `DoNotIncludeJars`, cost is negligible.

**Complements.** Gradle's `java-library` plugin keeps a module's compile classpath to what it declares (`api` leaks, `implementation` does not), so not declaring `core` at all makes the boundary a compile error before any test runs [7]. JPMS `module-info.java` is available (Gradle infers the module path) but adds ceremony for one invariant. Deptective, a javac-plugin alternative, still shows `1.0-SNAPSHOT` with no tagged release and is not a multi-year bet [8]. `jdeps` is a zero-dependency CI check that was not evaluated this run.

**Confidence.** High on versions and the rule DSL (two sources); medium on Gradle 9 (absence of evidence) and on the performance reconciliation (issue thread plus one practitioner report).

## 3. SPRT as Fishtest uses it, adapted to win rate and depth

**Question.** Which sequential test should the rig use for win rate, which for depth while win rate is near zero, and what bounds and sample sizes follow?

**Verdict.** Port Fishtest's GSPRT (the approximation, regularization, and clamp) to the rig and run it on paired-seed differences of an ordinal or continuous metric (depth reached, later a survival score) as the gating test until win rate is well above zero; report win rate with the same machinery's confidence interval but do not gate on it. State hypotheses in standardized units so one bound pair gives one run budget across metrics, pre-register the bounds and record a hypothesis id per test, and adopt two stages (a small seed set, then the standard set) like Fishtest's STC/LTC (short and long time control) stages. Note the overshoot tension in the ADR and keep the clamp, revisiting sequential boosting only if error rates measured in simulation drift.

**What Fishtest actually runs.** Not a textbook Wald SPRT but a Generalized SPRT, in five facts the E3 ADR will cite separately:

- *Test*: nuisance parameters are replaced by maximum-likelihood estimates and the log-likelihood ratio is approximated as LLR = (1/2)(phi1 - phi0)(2 phi_hat - phi0 - phi1) / V(phi_hat), where phi_hat is the unconstrained estimate of the quantity of interest and V its variance estimate; the test stops when LLR leaves [ln(beta/(1-alpha)), ln((1-beta)/alpha)] [54] [53] [55].
- *Unit of play*: a game pair with reversed colors scored on five outcomes (0, 1/4, 1/2, 3/4, 1), which captures the correlation inside a pair and saves testing resources over independent games [54] [53].
- *Bounds*: stated in *normalized Elo*, e_n = (800/ln 10) x t_n with t_n = (mu - 1/2)/sigma_pg, chosen so that the expected duration depends only on the bounds and not on the draw rate or book [55] [57].
- *Live code*: `sprt.py` and `LLRcalc.py` implement exactly this, with sqrt(2) scaling for pentanomial results, zero counts regularized to 1e-3 so the variance is never zero, and the LLR clamped against overshoot [56] [57]; fastchess ships an independent implementation of the same construction [60].
- *Duration*: the expected worst case at alpha = beta = 0.05 is D/(e_n,1 - e_n,0)^2 with D about 1.05 million games, which is why Fishtest's tests need tens of thousands of games for one or two normalized Elo [55]. Fishtest's current default bounds are shown as an image on the wiki and could not be quoted as text; historically STC and LTC used different bound pairs and were tightened when outcomes became less informative [59] [58].

**Win rate as a Bernoulli SPRT.** With increments ln(p1/p0) per win and ln((1-p1)/(1-p0)) per loss and bounds +/- ln 19, Wald's approximation for p0 = 0.05 versus p1 = 0.07 gives about 780 runs under H0 and 700 under H1 for a one-sample test against a known baseline [61] [62]. The lead's Monte Carlo (4,000 trials per arm) reproduced 808 and 727 with error rates of 4.6% and 4.8%, and Van den Bergh's Brownian formula gives about 750 at the endpoints and 1,220 at the midpoint [63] [55]. Two-sample comparisons roughly double the variance, so budget two to four times that. Near zero the test degrades: the loss increment vanishes while the win increment stays large, so a few lucky wins can end a test and overshoot is no longer negligible [61]. Fischer and Ramdas show Wald's approximate thresholds do not guarantee the nominal errors when beta > 0 and propose "sequential boosting" as a fix, whereas Fishtest simply clamps [64] [56]. A fixed-horizon test peeked at repeatedly inflates its false-positive rate, which is the argument for any pre-registered sequential rule [67].

**Depth and other per-run metrics.** The GSPRT approximation is metric-agnostic: for depth reached, phi is the mean, phi_hat the sample mean, V = s^2/N, and the test is a sequential t-test in the same 20 lines [54]. With paired seeds, the statistic is the per-seed difference d_i = depth_A - depth_B, whose variance is lower than the sum of the two arms whenever outcomes on a seed are positively correlated; this is the direct analogue of Fishtest's pentanomial pairing [54]. Under H1 the expected runs are about 2.65 x 2 sigma^2 / (phi1 - phi0)^2, so a 0.3-floor improvement with a paired-difference standard deviation of 2.5 needs on the order of 370 seed pairs, against thousands of runs for the same evidence from win rate while p is near zero (illustrative arithmetic; the standard deviations must be measured) [54] [55]. Anytime-valid alternatives exist for when the effect size is unknown or several metrics are watched at once: mixture SPRT (deployed in commercial A/B testing) and e-processes, which allow stopping at any time and reject at 1/alpha [66] [65]; an anytime-valid sequential-rank test appeared in 2025 but no off-the-shelf anytime-valid Mann-Whitney for heavily tied ordinal data was found [68]. A reference sequential t-test implementation exists in R [69]; no Java library was found, and porting `LLRcalc` is small.

**Confidence.** High on the GSPRT mathematics and the Fishtest implementation (primary notes and source read in full, second implementation agrees); high on the sample-size arithmetic (formula plus simulation plus a second formula); medium on the depth-metric numbers, which use placeholder standard deviations; low on Fishtest's current default bounds, which were not obtained as text.

## 4. Information-set and redetermination search

**Question.** Under the rule that search never sees hidden state, which lookahead design should E6 take, how many samples and how deep, and what are the known failure modes?

**Verdict.** Three fair designs, in order of cost:

1. one-ply expectimax over Codex-derived tables (no simulator);
2. depth-limited expectimax or POMCP-style sampled search over a redetermined engine snapshot, horizon 2 to 4 hero turns, budget split swept empirically;
3. ISMCTS only if a measured strategy-fusion problem appears, at 2 to 4x the per-iteration cost. Before committing to (2), measure leaf correlation and disambiguation for SPD tactics with Long et al.'s recipe and measure the simulator's turns per second. The search leak test (identical decisions across hidden-state variants) is the fairness gate for any of the three.

**The lineage and its pathologies.** Practical imperfect-information search descends from Maven in Scrabble (simulation over likely scenarios) [13] and GIB in bridge, which sampled hidden hands and solved each with a perfect-information solver [12]. Frank and Basin named the two structural errors of that PIMC approach: *strategy fusion* (assuming one strategy can serve every sampled world when different information sets need different actions) and *non-locality* (a node's value depends on more than its subtree) [10]. Information Set MCTS (ISMCTS; Cowling, Powley, Whitehouse 2012) builds one tree over information sets and samples a fresh determinization per iteration, which removes strategy fusion but not non-locality [14] [15]; Extended PIMC (2024) is the newest mitigation that keeps PIMC's structure and postpones perfect-information resolution deeper in the tree [16]. Whether ISMCTS beats plain determinization is game-dependent: it wins where shared-tree depth matters and loses in highly stochastic, multi-action domains; it also costs two to four times more per iteration [15].

**When plain redetermination is good enough.** Long et al. (2010) predict PIMC quality from three measurable properties: leaf correlation (probability sibling terminal nodes share a payoff), bias, and the disambiguation factor (how fast information sets shrink with depth). PIMC is worst when leaf correlation is low and disambiguation is mid-range; as disambiguation approaches 1 the game approaches perfect information and PIMC "improves drastically" [11]. The paper gives a recipe for measuring all three from random playouts, and calibration points: Skat and Hearts (leaf correlation 0.8 to 1.0, disambiguation about 0.6) sit where PIMC loses only about 0.1 points per game to a Nash player; Kuhn poker (disambiguation 0) is where PIMC fails [11]. Nobody has measured these for a roguelike. The lead's inference, low confidence: roguelike *tactics* have high disambiguation (each monster turn reveals type, damage, and item effects) and high late-fight leaf correlation, which predicts plain redetermination is not badly hurt; unidentified-*item* decisions are the poker-like regime and belong to belief reasoning, not tree search.

**The single-agent template.** POMCP (Silver and Veness 2010) is the reference algorithm for exactly Shatterfish's shape: a particle belief over hidden state, each simulation sampling one hidden state and running UCT through a black-box simulator; its pocman domain (a maze, chasing ghosts, partial observation, death penalty) is the closest published analogue to dungeon tactics [17]. DESPOT reports similar settings with 500 sampled scenarios and a depth-90 discounted horizon at one second per step [18]. Both require only a simulator, not explicit transition models, which matches an engine-backed redetermined simulator.

**Budgets in practice.** The ISMCTS authors use 10,000 iterations per decision as "roughly one second of computation" and show the determinizations-versus-iterations split matters: in LOTR: Confrontation, 1 x 10,000 beat 40 x 250 by 22.9 percentage points and reached average tree depth 8.6 versus 4.1; in Dou Di Zhu the split barely mattered [14]. Whitehouse's thesis confirms about one second per 10,000 iterations, the sensitivity to the split, and the practical horizon truncation of cutting simulations at a natural boundary with a hand-built value at the cutoff [15]. The design consequence: Shatterfish must sweep its own split on its own engine, and a simulator that cannot deliver thousands of simulated turns per second per decision cannot support this class of search at all.

**Abstract models.** No hand-built roguelike combat simulator with published accuracy or speed was found. Campbell and Verbrugge's NetHack combat work abstracts the *state and actions* (normalized stats, inventory tuples, nine primitives plus per-item actions, a prerequisite mask) but uses the real game as the transition model, and their learned policy beat a scripted baseline only in the weapons-only setting [19]. Recent imperfect-information work (GO-MCTS, LAMIR) confirms "sample belief, then depth-limited search" as the mainstream template but is learned-model or CFR-scale, not a solo-engineer drop-in [20] [21]. NetPlay's finding that long-horizon planning in NetHack is hard because the agent cannot know what will appear supports a short tactical horizon under a separate strategic layer [22]. Java ISMCTS implementations exist for reference [23].

**Confidence.** High on the lineage, pathologies, and published budgets (primary papers read); medium on POMCP/DESPOT numbers (one from search text); low on the roguelike-specific inference, which is explicitly the lead's and unmeasured.

## 5. The NetHack Challenge, AutoAscend, and the symbolic-bot tradition

**Question.** What did the strongest public roguelike bots do, what did the 2021 challenge prove about symbolic versus learned agents, and has anything changed since?

**The challenge.** The NeurIPS 2021 NetHack Challenge drew 483 entrants and 631 submissions across four tracks; the ranking metric was ascensions, then median score, then mean; no entry ascended [24]. The winner, AutoAscend, was symbolic, reached a median of about 5,300 ("only just above a Beginner score"), and beat the best neural entry's median nearly threefold; the organizers concluded that "symbolic bots currently outperform deep RL by a large margin" and that neural agents struggled with hierarchy and long-term relationships [24]. The best neural entry, RAPH, was itself a hybrid: a rule-based top-level policy over hand-coded skills with only the fighting skill learned [24] [27]. A 2023 reproduction puts AutoAscend at mean 8,556, median 4,918, mean dungeon level 3.1, and shows imitation learning from its games asymptoting far below it, attributing the gap to hierarchical control and hand-written parsers rather than data volume [25]. Nothing since has surpassed it: the 2026 BALROG leaderboard shows every LLM below 7% NetHack progression [28], and a January 2026 code-writing LLM harness reached at best 12.6% with no ascension, its author naming spatial reasoning and reactive play as the roadblocks [29].

**How AutoAscend is built.** A `Strategy` is a generator that yields its entry condition once before any action, so conditions are cheap and side-effect free. Combinators `preempt`, `until`, `before`, `repeat`, and `every(n)` compose them, and the global strategy is a linear preemption list (Sokoban, altar work, wait-out bad states, cure, eat, follow guard, fight, emergency) over an exploration default. The team calls it "a custom variation of a behaviour tree capable of dynamic tree expansion" [26] [24]. Exploration scores frontier cells by dilating unseen stone, penalizing searched cells quadratically, and adding bonuses for doors and dead ends, with an escalating anti-stall counter [26]. Combat is one-step heuristic scoring over 34 abstract actions (move, melee, ranged, zap in eight directions, Elbereth, wait) with hand-tuned priorities and one ray simulation for wands; the only monster memory is the previous frame's glyphs, so there is no persistent belief about unseen monsters [26] [24]. Item identification is an explicit glyph-to-object belief table. The repository is about 550 KB of Python, a third of it data tables, with the strategy framework itself under 6 KB [26]. Its README warns that custom input "may confuse the agent", so it assumes its own action stream [26].

**The tradition.** qw, the DCSS Lua bot, drives play from a user-editable goal list (branches, runes, gods, meta-goals) executed in order, with per-domain plan files and explicit flee logic that computes whether enemies gain distance; it was "the first bot to win DCSS with no human assistance" but "does not have a high winrate" [30]. The Angband Borg evaluates an ordered goal list, acts one step, observes, and repeats, using danger and flow maps tuned through a text file without recompiling [31]. BotHack (Clojure, Java API, no game modification) won NetHack without wizard mode in January 2015 and is archived [32]. Rog-O-Matic (1984) was a blackboard system of prioritized "experts" fed by sensory knowledge sources, resolved unknown items by experimentation against a hand-coded database with a long-term memory file, and outscored 15 expert humans on median [33].

**Lessons that transfer.**

1. Arbitration is a priority list of interruptible behaviors with cheap entry predicates; three of four bots use exactly that.
2. Combat can be one-step scoring over an abstract action set and still win the field; lookahead is a refinement, not a prerequisite.
3. Explicit belief tables for identification are universal; persistent belief about unseen monsters is rare (AutoAscend has none), which is a place Shatterfish can be better.
4. Goals and priorities as data (qw, Borg) are what let non-programmers tune a bot.
5. Bots that assume their own action stream break under human takeover; observation-driven re-planning is the fix.
6. Score-chasing entries died of starvation on level 1, so an optimization target must reward progress, not points [24].
7. Learned components entered at the tactical leaf under symbolic arbitration (RAPH), which is where E9 puts them.

**Confidence.** High throughout: the challenge report, the 2023 reproduction, and the bot repositories were read directly; the AutoAscend preemption order is medium (summarized from source, not line-verified).

## 6. The Shattered Pixel Dungeon ecosystem

**Question.** What already exists around SPD (bots, RL, seed tools, headless and fork precedent), how does upstream's cadence affect a pinned fork, and where does reliable mechanics lore live?

**Verdict.** Shatterfish enters an empty field: nobody has driven SPD headlessly for play, and the closest tools patch the game to run generation inside an invisible window. Pin at v3.3.8, expect a single large upstream drop for 4.0 with content-heavy rather than structural changes, and measure the merge with one API call when it lands. Treat the seed as fixing generation only until the code says otherwise, and treat both wikis as tier-3 lore pending `path:line` citations.

**Upstream.** The repository has about 6,500 stars and 1,600 forks, two named contributors, 10 open issues, GPL-3.0, and a README that states "this repository does not accept pull requests!" while offering the code for reuse [70] [71]. Minor versions land every three to six months (v3.0.2 2025-03, v3.1.1 2025-06, v3.2.5 2025-09, v3.3.8 2026-03) [72]. The `4.0.0-beta` tag published 2026-08-15 points at the same commit as v3.3.8 and the release page says "there is no public source code for beta releases"; upstream master has had zero commits since the week of 2026-03-22 after 291 commits in the preceding 28 weeks [73] [74] [75]. Evan develops 4.0 privately and will drop roughly six months of work at once; its announced contents are a new dwarven-city quest with a stealth enemy AI "currently only used in the new quest", art, enchantments, a challenge overhaul, and bugfixes, with no statement of restructuring; v3.3.8 already contains the quest's tester area [77] [78] [79]. Merge cost is therefore unmeasurable today and should be measured with one `compare` call when 4.0.0 stable appears.

**Seeds.** Custom seeds shipped in v1.3 (2022). Evan's definition is generation-only: "When the game generates the dungeon, a lot of purely random calculations are involved. If they're given a numerical 'seed', then these calculations always give the same result"; the post says nothing about combat rolls or identification either way; the same release also introduced multiple floors per depth [76]. Community tooling confirms the practical corollary: seeds are guaranteed only within a build, "likely, but not guaranteed" to match across patch versions [81] [92].

**Bots, RL, and headless precedent.** No working bot, TAS, RL agent, or gym environment for SPD or Pixel Dungeon exists: the only bot project is an abandoned university course fork with no results, and Gymnasium lists no Pixel Dungeon environment [84] [85]. The nearest precedent for driving the game's code without playing it is the seedfinder lineage: the original patched upstream source to run generation from a CLI and is archived. Its successors are a full fork at v3.3.8 with an in-game seedfinder and a CLI line whose boot recipe keeps the LWJGL3 application with `setInitialVisible(false)`, patching a dozen game classes to expose generation results, rather than using the headless backend [80] [81] [82]. A Rust reimplementation of the generator reports 147 seeds per second per JVM process for the game's own generator across 19 floors, the only throughput figure found (self-reported, single source) [83]. SPD forks keep up with upstream either by wholesale git merge (Experienced PD, which stopped at 2.5.x) or never (Radish, pinned at 1.4.3); a GitHub search for SPD mods sorted by stars topped out at 27 stars and mostly inactive repositories, while the largest known fork (Experienced PD) has 83 [86] [87] [88].

**Lore sources.** Two wikis exist: the Fandom wiki, which hosts vanilla Pixel Dungeon and Shattered pages on one site under prefixes and is the main vector for confusing the two games, and pixeldungeon.wiki, created 2025-06-28 to replace it, Shattered-focused, with 299 pages and Discord links [91] [89]. The new wiki carries formula-level mechanics that name code identifiers (`attackSkill`, `defenseSkill`) but cite no source file, so it is a useful hypothesis generator and never a citation [90]. Evan's blog remains the design-intent source; upstream links no Discord or subreddit, and 2026 posts direct discussion to Lemmy and the Steam forums [71].

**Confidence.** High on upstream facts (GitHub API and Evan's posts read directly); high on the absence of bots (two searches, two sources); medium on the seedfinder throughput figure (single, self-reported).

## 7. Noosa and SPD UI patterns for a docked panel

**Question.** Which native building blocks exist for an in-game docked panel, how do forks add HUD elements, what are the threading and input rules, and how is UI scale handled? All claims below were verified against the pinned tag v3.3.8 by reading the source; a first pass on upstream `master` (post-beta) found the same code in substance, with the HUD construction order corrected at the tag.

**Verdict.** The panel is a `Component` with `Chrome.TOAST_TR` background, `renderTextBlock` text, `RedButton` controls, and a `ScrollPane` log, added at the end of `GameScene.create()` and positioned in `layoutTags()`'s free column; hotkeys are one `SPDAction` constant each. Two hooks are required (adding the panel in `GameScene.create()`/`layoutTags()`, and the `SPDAction` constants) plus one more if the embedded driver must bypass sprite waits for speed. All panel writes go through `Game.runOnRenderThread`; nothing on the brain thread may touch a `RenderedTextBlock`.

**Scale and layout.** The UI draws through `PixelScene.uiCamera`, a fullscreen camera at `defaultZoom`. That zoom is read from the saved `SPDSettings.scale()` and only falls back to `gate(2, ceil(density x scaleFactor), maxDefaultZoom)` (scaleFactor 3.75 for the full desktop interface, 2.5 mobile) when the saved value is out of range, and in full UI it is raised to at least half the maximum [93]. World zoom (`minZoom`, `maxZoom`) is separate, so a panel sized in UI pixels is unaffected by the player zooming the dungeon [93]. There is no layout manager: every `Component` positions its children in `layout()` from `Game.width/height`, and `PixelScene.align()` snaps to `1/defaultZoom` to keep nine-patches and text crisp [107] [93]. Text comes from `renderTextBlock(text, size)`, which also folds in the device's real pixel scale [93]. `SPDSettings.interfaceSize()` is 0 (mobile), 1 (full layout without the inventory pane), or 2 (full with it), default 2 on desktop and forced to 0 when the window is smaller than twice the density-scaled full-UI minimum of 360 x 200 [106] [93].

**Building blocks and reserved space.** `Chrome.Type` offers 16 nine-patch skins from one atlas; `TOAST_TR` and `TOAST_TR_HEAVY` are the translucent HUD backgrounds, `WINDOW` (margin 6) the modal frame, `RED_BUTTON`/`GREY_BUTTON` the buttons [94]. `GameScene.create()` builds the world groups in fixed z-order and then the HUD: `MenuPane` (width 31), `StatusPane` (height 39 in full UI), `BossHealthBar`, the resume/action/loot/attack indicators, `GameLog`, `Toolbar` (height 26), and, only at interface size 2, `InventoryPane` (187 x 82) docked bottom-right with the toolbar lifted above it [95] [105]. `layoutTags()` stacks the indicator tags upward from the toolbar on the left or right per `flipTags()` and sizes the `GameLog` to 160 UI pixels minus the tag width or inset in full UI [95]. A right-docked panel therefore competes with the menu pane at the top right and the inventory pane at the bottom right; the free column is between them, or the panel replaces the inventory pane at interface size 1. `ScrollPane` clips with its own camera and already handles drag, wheel, and keyboard scrolling, which makes it the scrolling-log widget [104].

**Threading.**

- *Render thread*: `Game.render()` draws then steps, `update()` caps the frame delta at 0.2 s, and `Game.runOnRenderThread` is literally `Gdx.app.postRunnable` [96].
- *Actor thread*: a dedicated "SHPD Actor Thread" runs `Actor.process()`, which picks the next actor under `synchronized (Actor.class)`, waits on a `Char`'s sprite while it `isMoving`, and parks with `wait()` when nothing can act. `GameScene.update()` spawns it when nothing is processing, calls `notify()` on it at most 60 times a second, drains a `toDestroy` list so UI removed from the actor thread is freed on the render thread, and `onPause()` waits up to 500 ms for it before saving [97] [95].
- *Input seam*: when `Hero.act()` finds no `curAction` it calls `ready()` (which sets `ready = true` and calls `GameScene.ready()`) and returns false so the actor thread parks; the default cell listener then runs `if (hero.handle(cell)) hero.next()`; `Hero.interrupt()` clears the action and `GameScene.cancel()` clears resting [98] [95].
- **The trap**: `RenderedText.measure()` throws "Text measured from the actor thread" if called from the actor thread, and `RenderedTextBlock.text()` is not synchronized even though `build()` is, so panel text must be set on the render thread [103] [102].
- *Consequences*: a bot that drives the hero inside the real game runs at animation speed unless it also controls sprites, and every panel update must be posted to the render thread.

**Input.** Actions are `SPDAction` constants in a static `defaultBindings` map keyed by libGDX key codes with a parallel controller map; bindings load from and save to `keybinds.dat` as deltas from the defaults [99]. `KeyBindings` exposes `setAllBindings` and lookups but no `addKeyBinding`; registering a new action means adding the constant and its default binding, after which the settings window lists it automatically because it enumerates `GameAction.allActions()` [100] [101]. That is a small, contained hook.

**Forks.** No fork with a large in-engine docked panel was found. The one "overlay" fork does its big HUD as an external Godot companion app over an HTTP gateway and adds only small Noosa widgets in-game [108]. Every mod is a full-source fork, so the invasive edit of `GameScene.create()` is the norm. On stability: `GameScene.java` did not change between v3.3.7 and v3.3.8, `ui/` changed only in its changelog class, and the `4.0.0-beta` tag is the v3.3.8 commit, so the hook surface is stable until 4.0 stable lands [109].

**Confidence.** High throughout: every claim cites the pinned tag by line.

## Cross-dimension insights

- **The fairness rule and the engine's architecture point the same way.** Because turn resolution lives in sprite callbacks (section 1, contrary A) and search must not see hidden state (section 4), the same component serves both: a harness-owned headless scene that runs the *real* engine on a state the harness controls. The redetermined simulator E6 may need is the headless scene fed a scrubbed snapshot. Building E1 this way makes E6's expensive option cheaper instead of dearer.
- **Measurement is the bottleneck in three dimensions at once.** Pair correlation (section 3), Long et al.'s properties (section 4), and fast-forwarded scene throughput (section 1) are all unknown, all measurable with published recipes, and all gate roadmap estimates. E1 should ship all three numbers, which is stronger than the bootstrap prompt's "1,000 runs in seconds" done-when.
- **The overlay's thread rule is the harness's thread rule.** `RenderedText.measure()` throwing on the actor thread (section 7) and the actor thread's wait on `sprite.isMoving` (contrary A) are the same discipline seen from two sides: game objects live on their threads, and only immutable observations cross. The bootstrap prompt's "brain never touches game objects" is not just fairness; it is the only way the code does not deadlock.
- **Symbolic bots and Fishtest share a failure mode: the target becomes the metric.** NetHack entrants gamed score (section 5); a depth-gated rig invites diving (contrary B). The composite outcome and the survival-curve view from the brainstorm answer both.
- **Upstream's silence is a window.** With no public 4.0 source and a stable hook surface (GameScene unchanged since v3.3.7, ui/ changed only in its changelog class), the next six months are the cheapest time to land E1's hooks; the merge cost arrives all at once later and should be measured, not feared.

## Contrary evidence

Three fresh-context skeptics attacked the major conclusions with no access to the run's evidence. Their findings were weighed, not appended; each subsection says what survived, what was overturned, and what changed.

### Contrary evidence A: the headless harness

A fresh-context skeptic attacked the plan to boot the game on the headless backend with no scene and guard "a handful" of rendering touchpoints. The finding was read on upstream `master`; the upstream reader then verified it line by line at the pinned tag, and it stands. It overturns the plan as originally stated:

- **Turn resolution runs through sprite animations.** `Mob.doAttack()` and `Hero.actAttack()` call `sprite.attack(cell)` and return false; the hit, `spend()`, and `next()` happen in `onAttackComplete()`, which only `CharSprite.onComplete(Animation)` calls, which only the render thread's `scene.update()` reaches [127] [98] [126] [132]. Wand zaps resolve in a `MagicMissile` callback and thrown items in `MissileSprite.onComplete(Tweener)` [128] [129]. Game logic runs in callbacks at 30 `sprite.attack(cell, callback)` sites, 86 `sprite.operate` sites, and 52 `sprite.zap` sites [130]. With no scene those callbacks never fire and the hero never gets a next turn.
- **"A handful" is off by two orders of magnitude.** The pinned tree has 969 `sprite.` dereferences across `actors/` and `items/` with about 72 guard lines; `Mob.act()` and `Hero.ready()` dereference the sprite on every turn; `GameScene.`, `Sample.INSTANCE`, `GLog.`, and `CellEmitter` appear 420, 439, 583, and 174 times [130] [127] [98]. `GameScene` guards its own statics with `scene == null` on 43 lines, but `spellSprite()` and the `CellEmitter` helpers do not [95]. `Sample.play` and `GLog` are harmless without a scene; sprites and emitters are not.
- **The actor wait is safe only with null sprites.** `Actor.process()` waits on `sprite.isMoving` only when `sprite != null`, and `isMoving` is cleared in `onComplete(Tweener)` from the render thread, with thread interruption and one boss sprite's overrides as the only other releases [97] [126]. A null sprite never blocks; a live sprite that is never updated blocks forever.
- **Classloader isolation has a hard constraint.** The JNI specification states that the same native library cannot be loaded into more than one class loader [117]. The project's own 2020 deadlock between the render and actor threads shows the two threads share monitors on scene-graph objects [118], and the one throughput figure found for a small roguelike engine (about 400k turns per second in-process) says raw turn cost is not the ceiling; per-decision policy overhead is [119]. Isolation therefore means libGDX and its natives in a shared parent loader and only the game's classes per child, which keeps `Gdx.*` shared and isolates `Dungeon`/`Actor`; it is a design, not a drop-in.

**What survives.** The backend behavior, natives, versions, and the process-per-instance default in section 1 are unchanged, and the code offers a cheaper route than either sprite-patching strategy:

- Enabling facts: the bare `CharSprite()` constructor chain needs no GL; textures load through `Pixmap` from `Gdx.files` (native decode, no GL); only class-initializing `Texture` and calling `frame()` need GL or a texture; tweeners and animations fast-forward when `scene.update()` is driven with a large elapsed time [131] [132].
- Three strategies by upstream-edit count (the upstream reader's estimate): a **headless scene** (components in Recommendation 1) needs zero edits to actor or item code and a few hooks around `GameScene.create()`, `spellSprite()`, and `CellEmitter`, and real sprite subclasses behave as in the game; a `HeadlessCharSprite` needs about 20 overrides plus routing for 11 `sprite()` overrides and several subclass-typed calls; null sprites touch hundreds of upstream lines.
- Consequence: section 1's verdict is amended, the E1 throughput target becomes conditional on how fast a fast-forwarded scene update runs, and the touchpoint-audit story stays first with this digest as its starting inventory.

### Contrary evidence B: the rig statistics

A fresh-context skeptic attacked the recommendation to gate on a Fishtest-style GSPRT over paired-seed depth differences. Three findings survive and change the recommendation:

- **Pairing may buy little.** Fishtest's own analysis of about 150 tests found a within-pair correlation of only about -0.15 and a variance saving of roughly 15%, and warned that using the wrong variance model invalidates the test [110]. Common-random-number studies show the benefit collapses when the two trajectories diverge, which two different policies do from their first differing decision [111] [112]. The "few hundred pairs" figure in section 3 assumed a paired-difference standard deviation it labeled illustrative; the honest prior is chess's 15%, and the correlation must be measured on the rig before any budget is promised.
- **Small samples break the approximations.** GSPRT's guarantees are asymptotic and Fishtest exercises them at about 100,000 games per test [53]; sequential t-statistics are known to be inaccurate at small sample sizes, and a zero-inflated integer metric estimated from its first few dozen pairs is exactly that regime [113]. One simulation study found an SPRT reaching 73% power against 83% for a fixed-sample design under identical conditions, and notes that SPRT's performance depends on the true effect matching the hypothesized alternative [115].
- **The gated metric becomes the target.** The NetHack Challenge organizers documented entrants gaming the scalar score; a depth-gated bot has the mirror incentive to dive and die deeper (section 5). Bounds also drift with the outcome distribution and had to be re-tuned in Fishtest's history [114].

Not surviving: the claim that SPRT is the wrong family altogether. E-value and mixture-SPRT designs remove the need for a pre-registered alternative and permit continued testing [116], and section 3 already names them; they are an alternative to evaluate in the E3 ADR, not a refutation. The amended recommendation appears under Recommendations.

### Contrary evidence C: the brain architecture

A fresh-context skeptic attacked the symbolic-first recommendation. One headline finding was overturned on re-reading the source, three survive in weakened form, and one is a genuine caution:

- **Overturned.** The skeptic reported LLM agents reaching 40% NetHack progression on BALROG in February 2026. The leaderboard's LLM table has the columns "MiniHack | NetHack | Date"; the Gemini-3-Pro row reads MiniHack 40.0 ± 7.7, NetHack 6.8 ± 3.2 [28]. Section 5's figure stands. What survives is the slope: from about 1.6% in late 2024 to 6.8% in early 2026 with no game-specific engineering [120] [28], so "learned components are late" is a scheduling choice to revisit annually, not a law.
- **The symbolic champion is plateaued.** No agent of any architecture has ascended NetHack 3.6.6 or later; the one recorded bot ascension used an exploit since removed [121]. AutoAscend's rule network was opaque enough that a research team had to instrument it to recover its strategy labels [122], and the behavior-tree literature warns that such hierarchies become "intractable" as behaviors multiply and are static in unanticipated situations [123] [124]. Shatterfish inherits this cost; mitigations are playbooks as data, a strategy log the overlay can show, and per-epic retrospectives, none of which AutoAscend had.
- **Privileged input.** AutoAscend consumed the structured glyph and stats feed of the NetHack Learning Environment (NLE), a privileged semantic interface, whereas Shatterfish's rule restricts the bot to what a human could see [125]. This is a fair point about cost, not feasibility: the Observer builds a semantic observation from what the renderer draws, which is closer to NLE's feed than to pixels, but the perception and belief layer is work AutoAscend never did and E1 must budget it.
- **Two points already in section 4.** Determinization versus ISMCTS, and the unmeasured "high disambiguation" inference (marked low confidence there, with item identification assigned to belief reasoning); the skeptic strengthens the requirement that E6 measure before choosing.

Not surviving: any evidence that a learned or hybrid agent has beaten the symbolic bots on a roguelike (the skeptic searched and found none), or that a priority-list design was abandoned by anyone who built one.

## Recommendations

Each is bound to the decision and to the artifact that consumes it. The confidence basis follows in parentheses.

1. **Architecture spine, E1.** Specify the harness as a headless scene: harness-owned `Scene` with the Groups `GameScene` provides, a no-op `GL20`/`GL30` installed before any `Texture` class loads, atlases via `Pixmap`, desktop natives shipped, `updatesPerSecond = 0`, per-instance preferences directory, and a driver loop that fast-forwards `scene.update()` until `Actor.processing()` is false. First story: the touchpoint audit, seeded with the round-4 digest's inventory. (High confidence: verified at v3.3.8.)
2. **Architecture spine and roadmap risk, E1.** Parallelism is process-per-game; the classloader-isolation spike must put libGDX and natives in a shared parent loader and only game classes per child, and report measured turns per second as its exit criterion. Replace the "1,000 runs in seconds" done-when with three measured numbers: fast-forwarded turns per second, pair correlation on a smoke seed set, and Long et al.'s leaf correlation and disambiguation for tactics. (High on the JNI constraint; low on isolation feasibility.)
3. **Architecture spine, ADR for E3 statistics.** Port Fishtest's GSPRT (approximation 2.1, regularization, clamp) to Java; run it on paired-seed differences of a composite outcome ordered win, depth, turns; state bounds in standardized units and pre-register them with a hypothesis id; require a burn-in and validate realized error rates by simulation on the rig's own outcome distribution before any bound is trusted; re-calibrate bounds per upstream tag; report win rate with a confidence interval and never gate on it while near zero; evaluate an e-process or mixture-SPRT design as the alternative in the same ADR. (High on the mathematics; medium on the sample-size promise, which must be measured.)
4. **PRD non-functional requirements.** Fairness tests as listed in the bootstrap prompt plus a search leak test; reproducibility as (tag, seed, action list) with the seed defined as generation-only until the code says otherwise; headless throughput stated as a measured target after E1, not a guess; overlay responsiveness as "brain thinks off-thread, all panel writes posted to the render thread, no `RenderedTextBlock` touched off it". (High.)
5. **Architecture spine, brain.** Arbitration as a priority list of interruptible behaviors with cheap entry predicates; explicit belief tables for identification; persistent belief about unseen monsters (AutoAscend lacks it); one-step combat scoring over an abstract action set first; playbooks and priorities as data; a strategy log shown in the overlay; a scheduled annual review of the learned frontier. (High on the tradition; the plateau risk is acknowledged.)
6. **Architecture spine, ADR for E6 search.** Start with one-ply expectimax over Codex tables; then measure the simulator's speed and the Long et al. properties; choose between depth-limited sampled search (POMCP-style, horizon 2 to 4 hero turns, split swept) and ISMCTS on those numbers; keep item identification in belief reasoning, not search. (High on the literature; the roguelike inference is low and must be measured.)
7. **UX spec and architecture, E5.** Panel as a `Component` with `Chrome.TOAST_TR`, `renderTextBlock`, `RedButton`, and a `ScrollPane` log, added at the end of `GameScene.create()` and placed in `layoutTags()`'s free column; hotkeys as `SPDAction` constants; two hooks plus one for sprite-wait bypass if the embedded driver must run faster than animation speed. (High: cited by line.)
8. **Build and CI.** Bump ArchUnit to 1.5.0; keep the three boundary layers; add `jdeps` only if a fourth is ever wanted. (High.)
9. **Brief: feasibility and positioning.** No bot, RL agent, gym, or headless harness exists for SPD; the seedfinder lineage is the only precedent and it boots an invisible LWJGL3 window. Both wikis are tier-3 lore; Evan's blog is the design-intent source; the seed fixes generation. (High.)
10. **Roadmap.** Land E1's hooks before 4.0 stable; measure the 4.0 merge with `compare` when it appears; expect content, not restructuring. (High on the current facts; the release date is open.)
