### Contrary evidence: the rig statistics

A fresh-context skeptic attacked the recommendation to gate on a Fishtest-style GSPRT over paired-seed depth differences. Three findings survive and change the recommendation:

- **Pairing may buy little.** Fishtest's own analysis of about 150 tests found a within-pair correlation of only about -0.15, a variance saving of roughly 15%, and warned that using the wrong variance model invalidates the test [110]. Common-random-number studies show the benefit collapses when the two trajectories diverge, which two different policies do from their first differing decision [111, 112]. The "few hundred pairs" figure in section 3 assumed a paired-difference standard deviation it labelled illustrative; the honest prior is chess's 15%, and the correlation must be measured on the rig before any budget is promised.
- **Small samples break the approximations.** GSPRT's guarantees are asymptotic and Fishtest exercises them at about 100,000 games per test; sequential t-statistics are known to be inaccurate at small sample sizes, and a zero-inflated integer metric estimated from its first few dozen pairs is exactly that regime [113, 114]. One simulation study found an SPRT reaching 73% power against 83% for a fixed-sample design when the true effect differed from the hypothesized one [115].
- **The gated metric becomes the target.** The NetHack Challenge organizers documented entrants gaming the scalar score; a depth-gated bot has the mirror incentive to dive and die deeper (section 5). Bounds also drift with the outcome distribution and had to be re-tuned in Fishtest's history [114].

Not surviving: the claim that SPRT is the wrong family altogether. E-value and mixture-SPRT designs remove the need for a pre-registered alternative and permit continued testing [116], and section 3 already names them; they are an alternative to evaluate in the E3 ADR, not a refutation. The amended recommendation appears under Recommendations.
