### Contrary evidence: the headless harness

A fresh-context skeptic attacked the plan to boot the game on the headless backend with no scene and guard "a handful" of rendering touchpoints. The finding was read on upstream `master`; the upstream reader then verified it line by line at the pinned tag, and it stands. It overturns the plan as originally stated:

- **Turn resolution runs through sprite animations.** `Mob.doAttack()` and `Hero.actAttack()` call `sprite.attack(cell)` and return false; the hit, `spend()` and `next()` happen in `onAttackComplete()`, which only `CharSprite.onComplete(Animation)` calls, which only the render thread's `scene.update()` reaches [127, 98, 126, 132]. Wand zaps resolve in a `MagicMissile` callback and thrown items in `MissileSprite.onComplete(Tweener)` [128, 129]. Thirty `sprite.attack(cell, callback)` sites, 86 `sprite.operate`, and 52 `sprite.zap` sites carry game logic in callbacks [130]. With no scene those callbacks never fire and the hero never gets a next turn.
- **"A handful" is off by two orders of magnitude.** The pinned tree has 969 `sprite.` dereferences across `actors/` and `items/` with about 72 guard lines; `Mob.act()` and `Hero.ready()` dereference the sprite on every turn; `GameScene.`, `Sample.INSTANCE`, `GLog.`, and `CellEmitter` appear 420, 439, 583 and 174 times [130, 127, 98]. `GameScene` guards its own statics with `scene == null` on 43 lines, but `spellSprite()` and the `CellEmitter` helpers do not [95]. `Sample.play` and `GLog` are harmless without a scene; sprites and emitters are not.
- **The actor wait is safe only with null sprites.** `Actor.process()` waits on `sprite.isMoving` only when `sprite != null`, and `isMoving` is cleared solely in `onComplete(Tweener)` from the render thread [97, 126]. A null sprite never blocks; a live sprite that is never updated blocks forever.
- **Classloader isolation has a hard constraint.** The JNI specification states that the same native library cannot be loaded into more than one class loader (Oracle spec, section 1 sources). Isolation therefore means libGDX and its natives in a shared parent loader and only the game's classes per child, which keeps `Gdx.*` shared and isolates `Dungeon`/`Actor`; it is a design, not a drop-in.

**What survives.** The backend behaviour, natives, versions, and the process-per-instance default in section 1 are unchanged. The bare `CharSprite()` constructor chain needs no GL; textures load through `Pixmap` from `Gdx.files` (native decode, no GL) and only class-initialising `Texture` and calling `frame()` need GL or a texture; tweeners and animations fast-forward when `scene.update()` is driven with a large elapsed time [131, 132]. That makes a third strategy the cheapest by upstream-edit count: a **headless scene**, that is a harness-owned `Scene` that supplies the Groups sprites attach to, a no-op `GL20` installed before any `Texture` class loads, real texture atlases decoded by `Pixmap`, and a driver that calls `scene.update()` with a large elapsed time until `Actor.processing()` is false. The upstream reader's estimate: zero edits to actor or item code, a few hooks around `GameScene.create()`/`spellSprite()`/`CellEmitter`, and sprite subclasses that call back into game logic behave as in the real game because they are the real classes. The alternative, a `HeadlessCharSprite` with synchronous callbacks, needs about twenty method overrides plus routing for eleven `sprite()` overrides and several subclass-typed calls, and the null-sprite strategy touches hundreds of upstream lines. Section 1's verdict is amended accordingly, and the E1 throughput target becomes conditional on how fast a fast-forwarded scene update runs; the "headless touchpoint audit" story stays first, now with this digest as its starting inventory.
